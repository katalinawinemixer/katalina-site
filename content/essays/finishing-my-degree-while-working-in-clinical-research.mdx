In August 2026, I finished my B.S. in Applied Health Sciences at Eastern Florida State College. I completed it while working full time in clinical research.

Work and school shared a lot of the same questions. I used my clinical research experience to frame many of my papers. The work gave me a place to start: what I had seen in participant visits, laboratory workflows, research data, and regulatory documentation.

I wrote about wearable devices. I also wrote about maternal mortality in the United States compared with other countries, and how clinical research could reach local populations in San Francisco, with a focus on underserved communities. Those topics let me look beyond the tasks of a particular study and think about who research reaches and what it can help us understand.

That connection was useful. At work, I had responsibilities within a study and a reason to get the details right. In a paper, I had room to step back, examine a question, and make an argument. School gave me a different way to work with the experience I was gaining on the job.

One of the harder parts was keeping track of time zones. I was working in Pacific time, while my assignments and papers were due in Eastern time. A due date had to make sense in the time zone where I was actually doing the work.

What helped was creating a calendar and converting the school deadlines to Pacific time. I could see when something was due without having to do that conversion each time. It gave me a practical lesson in time management: account for the time zone when you make the plan, rather than leave it until the deadline is close.

I also worked ahead of my deadlines. At the beginning of the week, I planned and outlined my papers. I did the writing during the middle and later part of the week, then reviewed by the end of the week. The aim was to submit a day before the due date, with time set aside for review before I sent it in.

The connection between work and school helped with the subject matter. The calendar helped me make room to do the reading, organize the ideas, and finish the paper alongside a full-time job.

I’m grateful my degree includes that experience. It gave me a chance to connect the work I was doing with questions about health, technology, and access to research. And it feels good to update the line on my bio that used to say “one course away.” I finished.
