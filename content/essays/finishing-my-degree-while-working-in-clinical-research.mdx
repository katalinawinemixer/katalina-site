In August 2026, I finished my B.S. in Applied Health Sciences at Eastern Florida State College. I completed it while working full time in clinical research.

Those two things fit neatly on a resume. Doing them at the same time is harder to fit into a sentence.

Clinical research asks for attention. At PPD, my work included participant visits, consent coordination, laboratory assessments, and source data quality. Later, at the University of Washington, I worked on regulatory affairs for a portfolio of hematologic-oncology trials. Protocol and consent amendments, IRB approvals, and study records all needed careful follow-through.

School asked for attention too. A full-time job does not make an assignment deadline disappear. Being a student does not make the workday shorter. Both responsibilities have a claim on the same hours, and a calendar can only do so much about that.

I do not think that experience needs to become a story about being exceptionally productive. Working and studying at the same time is a practical problem: deciding what needs your attention now, what you can prepare ahead of time, and what will have to wait. The challenge is keeping up with both without treating every task as equally urgent.

There was also a useful connection between them. I was studying health sciences with clinical research experience already behind me. Questions about research and patient care had a practical context: a participant visit, a consent form, a laboratory sample, or a study record that someone else needed to rely on.

That is the background I bring to my next role. I have worked with participants, laboratory workflows, research data, and regulatory documentation. I also finished the degree alongside that work. I want to keep using that combination in roles where understanding how research happens is useful.

For now, it feels good to update a line that used to say “one course away.” I finished.
